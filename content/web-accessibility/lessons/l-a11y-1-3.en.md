"Is it accessible?" is a question with no answer until you agree on what accessible means. For web content, the agreed answer is the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)**, published by the W3C. Laws in Europe, the US and many other countries point to it, and audit reports are written in its language. You don't need to memorise it, but you do need to read it fluently.

## How WCAG is organised

WCAG has four layers, from broad to testable:

1. **Four principles**, remembered as **POUR**. Content must be **Perceivable** (users can sense it), **Operable** (users can work the controls), **Understandable** (users can make sense of it and predict it), and **Robust** (it works with current and future assistive technology).
2. **13 guidelines** under those principles, such as 1.1 Text Alternatives or 2.1 Keyboard Accessible.
3. **Success criteria** under each guideline: the testable rules. Each has a number, a name and a level: **A**, **AA** or **AAA**. For example, "1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), AA".
4. **Understanding documents and techniques** that explain intent and show known ways to pass or fail.

Conformance is claimed at a level, and each level includes the ones below it. **Level AA** is the target used by nearly every law and company policy, and it is the target for Ticketly. W3C itself advises against requiring AAA for whole sites, because some AAA criteria can't be met for all kinds of content.

:::tip Read the Understanding pages
The success criterion text is legal-style and terse. Each criterion links to an "Understanding" page that explains who it helps, with examples. When you are unsure whether something fails, that page usually settles it.
:::

## What changed in 2.2

WCAG 2.0 came out in 2008, 2.1 in 2018 and **2.2 in October 2023**. Each version is backwards compatible: a page that meets 2.2 also meets 2.1 and 2.0. Version 2.2 removed one criterion, 4.1.1 Parsing, because modern browsers handle broken markup consistently. It added nine. Six are at level A or AA, and every one of them catches a real Ticketly bug:

| Criterion | Level | Ticketly today |
|---|---|---|
| 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) | AA | The sticky cookie banner hides focused links at the bottom of the screen. |
| 2.5.7 Dragging Movements | AA | The price-range slider only works by dragging. |
| 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) | AA | The ticket quantity "−" and "+" buttons are 16 px squares, side by side. |
| 3.2.6 Consistent Help | A | The "Contact us" link moves between pages. |
| 3.3.7 Redundant Entry | A | Checkout asks for the email address again on step 3. |
| 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum) | AA | The account password field blocks paste. |

**Target size** deserves a closer look because it is easy to measure. A pointer target must be at least **24 by 24 CSS pixels**, unless it has enough spacing (a 24 px circle centred on it doesn't overlap another target), an equivalent control elsewhere meets the size, it sits inline in a sentence, its size is set by the browser, or that exact presentation is essential (a legally required form, say). Making Ticketly's quantity buttons 24 px squares, or spacing them apart, fixes it:

```css title=ticketly/styles.css
.qty button {
  min-inline-size: 24px;
  min-block-size: 24px;
}
```

**Accessible authentication** is the one that surprises teams. It says login can't depend on a "cognitive function test", such as remembering a password or transcribing a code, unless there is an alternative or a mechanism to help. Allowing paste and autofill so password managers work is usually enough. Blocking paste on a password field fails it.

:::mistake Treating WCAG as a checklist you run once
Teams often audit before launch, fix the report and move on. Six months later new components have reintroduced every problem. WCAG describes the state of a page at a moment in time; keeping that state requires the habits you will build in [Auditing a Page and Building an Accessible Team](lesson:l-a11y-5-3).
:::

## The law, briefly

This is the landscape in 2026, not legal advice; check with your own counsel for specifics.

- **European Union.** The **European Accessibility Act** (Directive 2019/882) has applied since **28 June 2025**. It covers products and services such as e-commerce, banking, e-books, transport ticketing and electronic communications, so a site like Ticketly selling to EU customers is in scope. Microenterprises providing services (fewer than 10 staff and no more than 2 million euros turnover) are exempt. The reference standard, EN 301 549 v3.2.1, maps to WCAG 2.1 AA for web content; version 4.1.1, published in September 2026, moves to WCAG 2.2 AA and becomes the reference once the EU cites it in its Official Journal. The older Web Accessibility Directive already required the same of public-sector sites.
- **United States.** Title III of the **ADA** covers businesses open to the public; it names no technical standard, but courts and settlements use WCAG AA, and thousands of web accessibility lawsuits are filed each year. A 2024 Department of Justice rule requires state and local governments to meet **WCAG 2.1 AA**; an April 2026 interim rule moved the deadlines to April 2027 for entities serving 50,000 people or more and April 2028 for smaller ones. Federal agencies follow **Section 508**, which references WCAG 2.0 AA.
- **Elsewhere.** Canada, the UK, Israel, Australia and others reference WCAG 2.0, 2.1 or 2.2 at level AA.

The practical conclusion is simple: build to **WCAG 2.2 AA** and you meet every one of these, because 2.2 includes everything in 2.1 and 2.0. WCAG 3.0 is in development as a W3C working draft with a different scoring model; it isn't a standard yet, and nothing in law requires it.
